### Section-by-section I-can and supporting-skill inventory

#### Chapter 1 - Graphs and Relationships

**1.1 - Scientific Investigations and Relationships**

- **PHY-LG-1.1**: I can investigate relationships between variables and support scientific conclusions with organized data, evidence, and reasoning. *(publisher: U1#1, U1#3)*
  - Identify the independent and dependent variables and what is kept consistent in a fair investigation.
  - Record measurements in a labeled data table and represent the pattern with a suitable graph.
  - Write a claim supported by specific measurements and a physics-based reason for the relationship.

**1.2 - Graphs, Patterns, and Scientific Models**

- **PHY-LG-1.2**: I can analyze data and graphs to identify relationships, interpret slope, and make evidence-based predictions. *(publisher: U1#2, U1#4)*
  - Identify the quantities and units on each graph axis.
  - Distinguish proportional, inverse, linear-but-not-proportional, and nonlinear trends.
  - Use observed data to evaluate whether a proposed relationship fits.
  - Calculate rise over run using two data points and include the correct units.
  - Explain what the slope represents for the measured quantities.
  - Predict a value from a graph or equation and compare it with available evidence.

#### Chapter 2 - 1-Dimensional Kinematics

**2.1 - Describing Motion with Words and Diagrams**

- **PHY-LG-2.1**: I can describe one-dimensional motion using measured quantities, direction, dot diagrams, and data tables. *(publisher: U2#1, U2#2)*
  - Distinguish scalars from vectors and average speed from velocity.
  - Determine the direction/sign of displacement, velocity, or acceleration from a situation.
  - Calculate simple average speed or velocity with meaningful units.
  - Interpret how dot spacing reveals speed and changes in speed.
  - Match a motion description to a data table or diagram.
  - Describe when acceleration can be present even if an object slows or reverses direction.

**2.2 - Position-Time Graphs**

- **PHY-LG-2.2**: I can construct and interpret position-time graphs to explain motion and determine velocity from slope. *(publisher: U2#3)*
  - Locate position at a given time and distinguish graph height from speed.
  - Interpret positive, negative, zero, and changing slopes as velocity.
  - Match a motion-detector or Pace Tracer observation to its position-time graph.

**2.3 - Velocity-Time Graphs and Motion Models**

- **PHY-LG-2.3**: I can interpret motion graphs and translate between representations to explain acceleration, displacement, and changes in motion. *(publisher: U2#4, U2#5)*
  - Describe rest, direction, speeding up, and slowing down from a velocity-time graph.
  - Determine acceleration from slope with appropriate units.
  - Find displacement from the signed area under simple velocity-time graph segments.
  - Match corresponding motion features across representations.
  - Sketch a compatible graph from a motion description or a different graph.
  - Explain a mismatch between two purported representations of the same motion.

#### Chapter 3 - Newton's Laws of Motion

**3.1 - Inertia, Balanced Forces, and Mass**

- **PHY-LG-3.1**: I can apply Newton's first law to explain how forces affect motion and distinguish mass from weight. *(publisher: U3#1, U3#2, U3#4)*
  - Distinguish a balanced force situation from one with a nonzero net force.
  - Relate inertia to mass without treating velocity itself as a force.
  - Predict motion qualitatively from the force balance and explain a common force-motion misconception.
  - Recognize mass as an inertial property and weight as a force.
  - Interpret a mass-versus-weight graph and its slope.
  - Use W=mg for a simple Earth-based comparison with units.

**3.2 - Recognizing and Representing Forces**

- **PHY-LG-3.2**: I can identify forces acting on an object and use free-body diagrams and net force to predict motion. *(publisher: U3#5, U3#6)*
  - Distinguish applied, tension, normal, gravitational, frictional, and air-resistance forces.
  - Show only forces acting ON the selected object and label directions.
  - Use opposing force magnitudes to identify balanced and unbalanced cases.

**3.3 - Acceleration, Air Resistance, and Interaction Forces**

- **PHY-LG-3.3**: I can use net force and mass to predict acceleration, including when air resistance changes during a fall. *(publisher: U3#3, U3#6, U3#7)*
  - Use cart-lab evidence to describe direct and inverse relationships among force, mass, and acceleration.
  - Use Fnet=ma to determine one unknown from the other two in simple cases.
  - Reason about acceleration direction from the direction of net force.
  - Describe how drag changes with speed or exposed area.
  - Interpret the sequence of forces and acceleration as a skydiver approaches terminal speed.
  - Use coffee-filter observations to support or challenge a terminal-speed model.
- **PHY-LG-3.3-B**: I can identify Newton's third-law force pairs and explain why the equal and opposite forces act on different objects. *(publisher: U3#8)*
  - Identify the two interacting objects before naming a force pair.
  - Distinguish interaction pairs from balanced forces on a single object.
  - Compare equal-and-opposite force magnitudes even when object masses or accelerations differ.

#### Chapter 4 - Free Fall and Projectiles

**4.1 - Free Fall and Vertical Motion**

- **PHY-LG-4.1**: I can explain and model vertical motion and free fall using gravitational acceleration, vectors, graphs, and data. *(publisher: U4#1, U4#2, U4#3)*
  - Recognize when gravity is the only significant force.
  - Identify the direction and approximate magnitude of gravitational acceleration near Earth.
  - Interpret video-lab evidence separating gravitational acceleration from differences caused by air resistance.
  - Explain changes in velocity before, at, and after the highest point.
  - Create or interpret free-fall motion diagrams, graphs, and data tables.
  - Use measured or modeled time patterns to estimate speed or vertical distance.

**4.2 - Projectile Motion Models**

- **PHY-LG-4.2**: I can predict ideal projectile motion by analyzing horizontal and vertical motion independently. *(publisher: U4#4, U4#5)*
  - Construct a trajectory and velocity-component diagram for a projectile.
  - Interpret horizontal and vertical motion tables separately.
  - Use the projectile simulation to test a prediction without assuming a forward horizontal force.

**4.3 - Projectile Predictions and Investigations**

- **PHY-LG-4.3**: I can predict a projectile's landing position from launch data and evaluate the prediction using experimental evidence. *(publisher: U4#6)*
  - Determine a useful launch-speed estimate from measured launch data.
  - Predict horizontal range from time of flight and horizontal speed in a simplified scenario.
  - Compare the single test launch with the prediction and identify plausible uncertainty sources.

#### Chapter 5 - Momentum and Collisions

**5.1 - Momentum and Impulse**

- **PHY-LG-5.1**: I can use momentum and impulse to explain how mass, velocity, force, and contact time affect changes in motion. *(publisher: U5#1, U5#2)*
  - Identify momentum direction from velocity direction.
  - Use p=mv and appropriate momentum units in simple physical contexts.
  - Predict how changing mass or speed changes momentum.
  - Relate impulse to average force, contact time, and change in momentum.
  - Compare collisions with the same momentum change but different stopping times.
  - Explain airbag, padding, catching, and balloon-toss examples using force-time reasoning.

**5.2 - Conservation of Momentum**

- **PHY-LG-5.2**: I can use interaction forces and conservation of system momentum to analyze and predict explosion outcomes. *(publisher: U5#3, U5#4)*
  - Compare action-reaction forces, contact times, impulses, and momentum changes.
  - Explain why equal and opposite momentum changes need not mean equal velocity changes.
  - Use a cart experiment to show opposite momentum directions.
  - Define the system and distinguish internal interactions from external effects.
  - Construct a before-and-after momentum table with signs.
  - Use total-momentum conservation to predict an unknown mass or velocity.

**5.3 - Collisions and Applications**

- **PHY-LG-5.3**: I can use conservation of momentum and proportional reasoning to explain and predict sticking and rebounding collisions. *(publisher: U5#5, U5#6)*
  - Track the momentum of both objects, including direction, before and after contact.
  - Use a collision table or diagram to compare total system momentum.
  - Predict an unknown post-collision velocity in a simple two-object case.
  - Identify the moving mass and initially stationary mass.
  - Compare final speeds when the mass ratio changes.
  - Explain Fish Catch outcomes without assuming momentum disappears when objects stick.

#### Chapter 6 - Work and Energy

**6.1 - Work and Forms of Energy**

- **PHY-LG-6.1**: I can determine when work is done on an object and explain how force and displacement transfer energy. *(publisher: U6#1)*
  - Identify when displacement and force create positive, negative, or zero work.
  - Use a simple force-distance relationship with joules and newtons.
  - Interpret evidence from an incline or plow investigation.
- **PHY-LG-6.1-B**: I can identify, calculate, and compare kinetic, gravitational potential, and total mechanical energy. *(publisher: U6#2)*
  - Relate kinetic energy to mass and speed and gravitational potential energy to height.
  - Determine total mechanical energy from KE and PE.
  - Use data to describe how KE and PE change as an object moves.

**6.2 - Conservation of Mechanical Energy**

- **PHY-LG-6.2**: I can use conservation of mechanical energy to predict changes between kinetic and gravitational potential energy. *(publisher: U6#5)*
  - Choose an appropriate system and compare KE+PE before and after motion.
  - Use a roller-coaster model to predict higher versus lower speeds.
  - Explain when an ideal conservation model is appropriate and when it is limited.

**6.3 - Energy Transfers and Representations**

- **PHY-LG-6.3**: I can use system boundaries and work-energy bar charts to explain energy transfers, including the effects of friction. *(publisher: U6#3, U6#4, U6#6)*
  - Distinguish conservative energy transformations from net nonconservative work.
  - Describe the fate of mechanical energy when friction acts.
  - Use energy-accounting evidence rather than claiming energy is destroyed.
  - Represent KE, PE, and relevant transfers on a bar chart.
  - Determine whether bar-chart values are consistent with energy accounting.
  - Connect chart representations to physical motion or simulation evidence.

#### Chapter 10 - Vibrations, Waves, and Sound

**10.1 - How Waves Work**

- **PHY-LG-10.1**: I can model vibrations and waves using period, frequency, amplitude, wavelength, and speed to explain energy transfer. *(publisher: U10#1, U10#2, U10#3, U10#4)*

**10.3 - Sound and Resonance**

- **PHY-LG-10.3**: I can model sound as a pressure wave and explain how its properties relate to pitch and loudness. *(publisher: U10#7, U10#8)*

  - Identify frequency from a microphone/audio recording and relate it to pitch.

- **PHY-LG-10.3-B**: I can explain resonance using natural frequency and observations of introductory standing-wave patterns. *(publisher: U10#6, U10#9)*

  - Identify increased response near a resonant frequency.
  - Recognize introductory nodes/antinodes without requiring full string or air-column harmonic mathematics.
